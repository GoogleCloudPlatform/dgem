🎙️ Part 1 — What is a decision model?

"Classifiers are the backbone of software decision-making. Traditional ML is fast, but needs thousands of labeled examples every time categories change. Autoregressive LLMs give us zero-shot flexibility, but they generate text one token at a time from left to right. DiffusionGemma introduces a third path: as you see in DeepMind's animation, discrete diffusion resolves tokens in parallel across the entire canvas."

What is a decision model?

Start here
1. You describe the situation and the questions.
A ticket, a record, a document or an image, plus a few multiple-choice questions: Which team? Is it urgent? How severe, 1–5?
2. It answers every question at once.
Instead of writing a reply word by word, the model returns a probability for every allowed answer, for every question, in a single pass. It can only pick from the options you listed.
3. Your software decides what to do.
When the model is clearly sure, act automatically. When it hesitates, ask a person or a bigger model. The rest of this walkthrough shows how to tell the difference.

How DiffusionGemma fills in text (Google DeepMind)

All at once
Instead of typing left to right, the whole page sharpens at once. dgem uses this to fill every answer blank together.

Three ways software makes these decisions

No training needed + a probability per answer

1. Traditional classifiers (e.g. BERT, XGBoost)

Very fast · Needs labeled data

Fast and cheap, but needs thousands of labeled examples and retraining every time you add or change a category.

2. Chat models (asked to reply in JSON)

Seconds · Confidence hidden

Works from plain-English instructions, but writes its answer word by word. Early words can steer later fields, and the text doesn't show whether it was 99% sure or guessing 51/49.

3. Decision models (dgem + DiffusionGemma)

One pass · Probability per answer

Works from plain-English instructions like a chat model, but answers all questions together in one pass and returns a probability for every allowed answer, plus a hesitation score. It is typically much faster than writing the answer word by word, and larger multi-question requests take longer than short ones.

⚠️ Hand off: hesitation 57% is 16% or more

Confident answers (like urgent = yes) are kept, and the uncertain one (department) is handed to Gemini along with dgem's odds (Technical 76%, Billing 23%) as a hint.

Low hesitation isn't a guarantee. Models tend to favor whichever option is listed first, which can make a coin flip look certain. dgem re-asks benchmark questions in shuffled order on every release and reports the extra flips; correcting each request inside the pass did not help reliably in our tests. Why a yes/no slot can't leak secrets

Only listed answers

A chatbot can write any of its ~262,000 word pieces. For each question, dgem only reads the probabilities of the allowed answers, like a stencil laid over the output. For injection_detected that's just "yes" and "no"; everything else is ignored, so there's no way to write out secrets through the answer. An attacker can still try to push the yes/no the wrong way, so test detection on your own examples (dgem bench-calibration includes prompt-injection items). The examples below are illustrative:
